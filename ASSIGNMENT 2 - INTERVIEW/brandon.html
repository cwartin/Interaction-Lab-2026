Conversations with Calista
From the perspectives of Chinese-Indonesians
“Please describe what feels like home to you?”
B
C:
The smell of nasi kuning usually means my mom is getting ready for a crowd.

She moves around the house fixing little things that nobody else would notice,

straightening the tablecloth,

rearranging chairs, making sure the house looks good enough for our extended family to take over.

By the time everyone arrives, the house is loud. Someone is always talking over someone else,
plates are being passed around, and my mom is insisting that everyone eat more.

I know these moments so well that I barely notice them when they happen. It is only when I am away that I realize how much I miss them.

In the home office, my dad’s iMac

holds another version of our family.

We crowd around it to watch old videos,

laughing at younger versions of ourselves doing things we no longer remember.

I sometimes stop paying attention to the video
and look around the room instead.

The same furniture is
there.

The same walls.

Almost the same house.


It is strange to realize that while I have grown up and spent years away, this place

seems to have stayed frozen in time, waiting for me to come back.
Coming home for summer means


finding out that some things really do not change.

I meet my friends at someone’s house,

and within minutes we are laughing so hard that my stomach hurts.

We talk about things that would probably sound completely unfunny to anyone else.

Then we end up on a basketball court,
running hoops like we did in high school.

The jokes are the same. The arguments over fouls are the same. Somehow, after months apart, we fall right back into place.
My Indonesian

is another story.


After being away for a while, talking to my grandparents can feel like trying to solve a puzzle

with half the pieces missing.

I forget words,
stumble through sentences,
and occasionally give up
and switch to English.


They laugh, I laugh,

and somehow we understand each other anyway.

Their voices make me realize how much of home lives in things I never thought I would lose, the language I grew up hearing, even when I can no longer speak it perfectly.
Then there are the people I have missed most.


When I finally see my long-distance girlfriend,

I hug her and notice something I have not been able to see through FaceTime: the freckles on her face.

For months,

she had been reduced to a screen,
frozen pixels, and a bad internet connection.

Suddenly, she is standing in front of me. I can see her clearly.

I can hold her.

It feels almost ridiculous that something so small could make me so happy.
Maybe that is what home
is for me.

It is not one house,
or even one city.

It is nasi kuning
on a crowded table,

an unchanged living room,
laughing until my stomach hurts,
struggling through Indonesian with my grandparents,

seeing my girlfriend’s freckles in person.

Home is familiarity.

It is the people and places
that let me stop performing, stop adjusting, and simply be myself. It is the feeling that, for once, I do not have to wonder where I belong.
